# 02 — Writing Samples: Prompts

> **Entity type:** `WritingSample` (corpus, role: prompt)
> **Source:** Raw user messages — Travis writing TO an AI agent.
> **Use:** Primary stylometric substrate. These are unedited Travis voice in his most natural register (informal-directive). Highest signal for diction, sentence rhythm, and lexical preferences.

The samples below preserve original capitalization, punctuation, and run-on structure. Annotations (in italics under each sample) flag stylistic signatures.

## Sample 1 — Initial ask (zed-workspace)

> i want to totally replace all my vscode based development tools with zed, and the most important part of that is workspace support that zed and all the other acp-driven ai tools will understand. that is the only feature that zed seems to be completely without

*Signatures:*
- Lowercase opening ("i want") even on a serious request
- Compound sentence joined with "and"
- Casual deictic closing ("that is the only feature...")
- Concrete and total: "totally replace all"
- Direct identification of the gap, not the wish ("the most important part of that is...")

## Voice Register (Two Modes)

Travis writes in two clearly distinguishable modes:

**Mode A — Casual Directive** (informal, fast, lowercase-friendly):
Used for: starting requests, redirects, quick corrections, mid-stream adjustments.
Example: *"i want to totally replace all my vscode based development tools with zed..."*

**Mode B — Architect Specification** (formal, structured, properly cased):
Used for: stakeholder-facing artifacts, brand documents, brief drafts, anything that will be read by Randy / Neil / Brittney / etc.
Example: *"Citizens National will have a named team — not a rotation of junior resources."*

The shift between modes is **context-dependent, not effort-dependent.** Travis is not "trying harder" in Mode B. Both are equally authentic. The substrate extractor must capture both.

### How the two writing modes adapt for a named human recipient

The real emails show that the Mode A / Mode B distinction holds in correspondence — but the prior derived model was wrong about several specifics. The samples are warmer, retain more of Travis's prompt-mode signatures, and use a consistent monogram sign-off the derived model did not predict.

**Mode A (Casual Directive) → Correspondence informal (working partner):**
Front-loading and direct-request structure carry over. The opener goes to the point quickly — but there IS a one-line warmth token before the business ("Thanks for sending that $500, as that helps a lot."). Not relational throat-clearing — a specific, concrete acknowledgment. The sentence rhythm stays fast. Run-on chains with "and" are retained. ALL-CAPS emphasis is retained ("ONLY", "BOTH", "JUST"). Self-critique appears mid-paragraph ("even though this goes against much of my perfectionist nature"). The email may close on a personal anecdote rather than an action item (the college-roommate paragraph in Email 1).

**Mode B (Architect Specification) → Correspondence formal (enterprise client):**
Mode-B density is preserved: full sentences, proper casing, structured paragraphs. Contrary to the prior derived model, the "etc." closer is RETAINED in formal email ("regardless of method (e.g., email, SMS, etc.)"). Parenthetical chains are retained. The close lands on a specific next step or a concrete readiness signal ("You will know that it is ready when you receive a nicely formatted and branded invitation email…"). A light warmth token opens ("Good morning, Hal!").

### What transfers from writing prompts to email

| Prompt feature | Transfers to email? | How it adapts (corrected against samples) |
|---|---|---|
| Front-loading context | Yes | Leads with the situational anchor, after a one-line warmth token |
| Explicit sequencing | Yes | Becomes structured sub-headers in estimate emails ("What Is Left", "Portal Side", "Agent Side") |
| "etc." as closer | **Yes — both tiers** | Retained in formal email, contrary to the prior derived model |
| Run-on chain with "and" | Yes (informal) | Retained in working-partner email; reduced in formal |
| ALL-CAPS for emphasis | **Yes** | Retained ("ONLY", "BOTH", "JUST") — the prior derived model wrongly predicted it drops |
| Backticked paths / tool names | Drops | Tool and file references not backticked in email prose |
| Parenthetical examples chained | Yes | Retained in both tiers |
| Hard-stop short sentence | Yes | Retained in all tiers |
| Self-critique mid-paragraph | **Yes (new)** | "even though this goes against much of my perfectionist nature" — a real correspondence feature |

## Short-Form Social Register

> _Extracted from 5 real Travis LinkedIn posts (2026-05-19). Posts 1–4 are the short-form core (link share, rant, humor, quote share); post 5 is a long-form written-social manifesto used here only for written-social cross-signal. The prior derived model framed this as a "≤280-character ultra-short" register — the samples correct that: Travis's short social posts run longer than tweet length. The register is short-form social, not strictly character-bounded._

### Register definition correction

The prior derived model assumed a sub-280-character constraint borrowed from Twitter/X. The actual samples are LinkedIn posts that run from one sentence (Post 1, Post 4) to several short paragraphs (Post 2, Post 3). The operative constraint is **single-thought brevity**, not character count: a short-form post carries one idea, one rant, one quote, or one announcement — not a layered argument. Length follows the thought; it is not capped.

### What a short-form post carries

| Element | Present? | Notes from samples |
|---|---|---|
| Hard-stop short sentence | Yes — core unit | "You gotta be kidding me..." / "I cussed that bot out GOOD!" |
| Single-thought scope | Yes | One announcement (P1), one rant (P2), one humor beat (P3), one quote (P4) |
| Specific detail over abstraction | Yes | "written in Rust", "Cedar policies", "CLAUDE.md and AGENTS.md" |
| ALL-CAPS for emphasis | Yes | "NO idea", "ANYTHING", "GOOD" — retained from prompt register |
| Double-hyphen "--" as em-dash | Yes | "anything--AI or otherwise--in Python" |
| Self-deprecating humor | Yes | "Good thing bots don't have an HR to run to" |
| Trailing ellipsis "…" as closer | Yes — characteristic | "get serious about agents…", "quote of the day…", "start to finish..." |

### Characteristic short-form openers (extracted)

- **One-line frame + link:** A single framing sentence preceding a shared link. *("It's time to get serious about agents…")* — Post 1.
- **ALL-CAPS label opener:** A bracketed or capitalized label announcing the post type. *("RANT OF THE DAY:")* — Post 2.
- **In-medias-res anecdote:** Opens directly in a story already underway. *("I had to cuss out one of my coding bots this morning…")* — Post 3.
- **Quote-first:** Opens with the quoted material, attribution follows. *(Galatians 6:9 quote, then "Our quote of the day…")* — Post 4.

### Characteristic short-form closers (extracted)

- **Trailing ellipsis:** The most common closer — signals an unfinished thought left deliberately open ("…").
- **Attribution-line sign-off:** An em-dash or double-hyphen attribution on its own line. *("--Worst AI Boss Ever", "—Galatians 6:9")*.
- **Punchline hard-stop:** A short declarative that lands the humor or the frustration ("You gotta be kidding me...").

### What doesn't appear in short-form posts (confirmed against samples)

- No "I'm excited to share..." openers
- No hashtag stacks in short-form posts 1–4 — hashtag stacks appear only in long-form post 5; the short-form posts carry zero hashtags
- No rhetorical questions where the answer is "yes" ("Isn't it time we...?")
- No calls to action that ask for validation ("thoughts?" / "what do you think?")

### Written-social cross-signal (from long-form Post 5)

Post 5 is not short-form — it is a long-form written-social manifesto. It is retained here only as cross-signal for the written-social register: numbered strategic lists, "But here's where we flip the script" as a pivot, a closing rhetorical challenge ("Who's ready to build the uncensorable future?"), and a hashtag stack at the end. These are written-social long-form features, distinct from the short-form patterns above. The short-form register does not inherit them.

## Spoken/Conversational Register

> _Extracted from a real ~21-minute recorded conference talk — "Identity + AI = Intelligent Applications," Ory Summit 2023 (published on the Ory Corp YouTube channel, 2024-02-08). This is a delivered, recorded talk — the highest-signal spoken substrate available. The patterns below are extracted from that transcript and confirmed against actual delivery._

### How documented written patterns surface in speech

Speech is not prose read aloud. The transcript confirms that some written patterns strengthen in speech, some convert to analogs, and some disappear.

**Patterns that strengthen in speech (confirmed):**
- **Specific numbers as anchors** — "roughly 35 minutes of manual workflow involving CDs, file copies, and imports." Numbers create an anchoring moment in delivery; Travis's written habit of citing exact figures translates directly.
- **Architecture anthropomorphization** — present but lighter than in writing. Travis uses it ("Users must become the sovereign owner of their own data") without leaning hard into dramatization.
- **Hard-stop pivots** — the written period becomes a vocal pause. "The future isn't necessarily about trillion-parameter models. It's about smaller models, purpose-tuned models, task-specific models."

**Patterns confirmed in delivery:**
- **Self-deprecating opener aside** — Travis opens warm and self-deprecating: "the home of the world's favorite sports franchise, the Dallas Cowboys. *(Sorry. Yeah, they're like number three or four, you know.)*" The parenthetical aside is a real spoken move.
- **"But seriously," pivot** — Travis pivots from humor back to substance with an explicit marker: "But seriously, I love the Ory product." The humor-then-substance transition is signposted aloud.
- **Mid-thought entry** — "You'll see where this leads to some interesting questions when it comes to the use of identity." Travis enters thoughts in motion rather than prefacing them.
- **Sequenced delivery without numbered lists** — "There are two very important parts of our platform…" Spoken structuring uses sequence language, not visible enumeration.
- **Flat personal-stake disclosure** — "This is very personal for me because I have a 23-year-old son who is schizophrenic." Delivered plainly, no dramatization. The weight is in the fact, not the framing.
- **Informal idiom in a formal talk** — "the 'big hairy goal' is enabling healthcare for everybody." Travis allows colloquial idiom even in a conference-talk context.

**Patterns that disappear in speech:**
- Backticked paths and tool names — not spoken
- Numbered list structure — replaced by sequence language ("first... then... after that...")
- Mode B's full sentence density — spoken mode naturally compresses

### Register-conditional exception — the Q&A-handoff close

The talk closes with: "Those are the things we're working on. If you have any questions, I'd like to field some."

The written-register rejection filter bans invitational closes ("Let me know your thoughts", "Happy to discuss"). **This ban does not apply to the spoken register.** The Q&A-handoff close is the standard, expected convention for ending a delivered talk — it is not a voice violation. The spoken register permits the Q&A-handoff close; the written register does not. When generating spoken content (talk prep, podcast outlines), a Q&A-handoff close is correct. When generating written content, it remains banned.

### Spoken-register behavioral markers

- **Forward-projection close on substance segments:** Spoken segments close on a stake or a next move. "Zephyr in particular is approaching GPT-4 and Claude performance levels."
- **Direct register as default:** Even in conversational delivery, Travis doesn't pad. No relational throat-clearing before the point.
- **Burst cadence:** Travis thinks in connected blocks — the transcript moves through architecture, then use case, then forward projection in continuous motion.

### What informs the spoken register from 10-collaboration-style

- **Strategic fight style:** In Q&A or debate contexts, Travis assembles the case analytically. He doesn't get reactive — he goes more precise.
- **"What specifically?" before answering vague questions:** In a spoken Q&A, if the question is vague, Travis surfaces the vagueness rather than answering the implied intent.
