# Authentic Digital Twin Content Standard v1

**Status:** v1.0 — published 2026-05-17. Subject to revision.
**Owner:** the author publishing under the standard. Each implementation declares its version inline.

---

## What the standard solves

Modern published writing is increasingly co-produced by a human author and one or more AI tools. Readers cannot tell, from the surface of an article, which sentences are the author's, which were drafted by AI and edited, and which are pure AI verbatim. This invisibility erodes trust in two ways: readers who suspect AI involvement discount the entire piece, and readers who do not suspect it absorb AI-generated claims as if they carried the author's certification.

The standard makes the boundary explicit. Every block of substantive content declares its authorship. The reader can audit which ideas are the author's and which are AI-assisted unedited.

This is not a disclosure of "AI was used." That disclosure is insufficient — it does not tell the reader *which* parts. The standard is block-level provenance.

---

## The three authorship categories

| Category | Tag form (italic eyebrow) | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| **Human-authored** | `_— <Author Name>._` | Original prose by the named author. No AI involvement, or AI involvement limited to spell-check and grammar that did not alter voice. The author certifies that the ideas, framing, sentence rhythm, and word choices are theirs. |
| **AI-drafted, human-edited** | `_— <Author Name> ← AI: <Provider> <Model> via <Tool>._` | Drafted by the named AI model via the named tool. The human author then edited the draft substantively for voice, framing, accuracy, and structure. The published voice is the human's; the provenance of the original draft is reported for transparency. |
| **AI verbatim** | `_— AI verbatim: <Provider> <Model> via <Tool>._` | Generated by the named AI model via the named tool and reproduced without editorial intervention. Used when the machine-default register is the correct one for the content — typically code samples, structured data outputs (JSON, YAML, TOML), and mechanical reference tables. |

The three categories form a partition. Every block belongs to exactly one. There is no "lightly edited" or "mostly AI" tier — the human author either certifies the published voice as theirs (`Human-authored` or `AI-drafted, human-edited`) or they explicitly do not certify it (`AI verbatim`).

---

## What counts as "editing" for the `AI ← edited` category

The category is meaningful only if the editing was substantive. The author must have done at least one of:

- **Voice rewriting** — changing sentence structure, word choice, or rhythm to match the author's substrate
- **Framing changes** — changing the order in which ideas appear, the rhetorical move chosen, or the depth claim made
- **Accuracy corrections** — verifying claims, adding citations, removing unsupported assertions
- **Structural reshaping** — adding or removing sections, changing paragraph boundaries

A pass that fixed a typo and added an em-dash is **not** substantive editing. If the draft was kept essentially as-is, the block is `AI verbatim`, not `AI ← edited`. The category is a certification, not a participation trophy.

## Block boundaries

A "block" under v1 is one of:

- A top-level prose section under a `##` heading (or `###` subsection when subsections are content-distinct)
- A code fence (`` ``` `` block) regardless of language
- A standalone reference table that exists as substantive content rather than as inline formatting
- A standalone JSON / YAML / TOML output that exists as a content example

Inline code, inline formatting, and footnotes within a prose block inherit that block's category. The eyebrow tag does not have to repeat for inline elements.

When a prose section contains both human-authored prose and an embedded code block of different provenance, the prose carries its own eyebrow at the section start, and the code block carries its own eyebrow immediately before the fence. The article in `zed-workspace-article.md` demonstrates this pattern in the "What we built" section.

## The provenance manifest

Every article that uses the standard ends with a **content provenance manifest** — a table that enumerates every block, its authorship category, and (for AI-involved blocks) the model provider, model, and tool.

Minimum manifest schema:

| Column | Required | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Section | yes | Match the section heading or a short label for the block |
| Authorship | yes | One of: `<Author Name>`, `<Author Name> ← AI`, `AI verbatim` |
| Model | when AI involved | Provider + model name (e.g., "Anthropic Claude Opus 4.7") |
| Tool | when AI involved | Authoring tool (e.g., "Claude Code", "Cursor", "GPT desktop app") |

Manifest entries are listed in the order the sections appear in the article. Multiple blocks within a section get their own rows if they belong to different categories.

The manifest must be human-readable. A reader who skipped the body and read only the manifest should be able to tell which sections to trust as the author's voice and which to read as AI output.

A short definitions block follows the manifest table, explaining the three categories. The definitions are repeated in every article rather than referenced externally — readers should not need to chase a link to understand the standard.

## Why the eyebrows are italic and on their own line

Three reasons:

1. **Scan-readability.** A reader skimming the article should be able to tell each block's category without breaking their reading flow. An italic eyebrow above the block is in the peripheral-vision range; an inline parenthetical tag is not.
2. **Markdown portability.** Markdown rendered to HTML, PDF, or Medium preserves italic-line typography reliably. HTML comments, color-coded backgrounds, and custom CSS classes do not.
3. **No information loss in plain text.** When the article is copy-pasted into a plain-text editor, the eyebrows remain readable as `_— Travis James._` rather than disappearing into formatting metadata.

Alternative annotations were considered and rejected:

- *Inline subscript footnotes* — break reading flow; reader has to chase the reference
- *Color-coded paragraph backgrounds* — not portable across renderers
- *HTML comments only* — invisible to the reader; useful only for the author's tooling
- *A single banner at the article top* — fails the block-level granularity requirement

## Edge cases and how the standard resolves them

**Edge case 1: A code block was AI-generated but the human author added a comment explaining it.**

The code itself is `AI verbatim`. The explanatory prose around it is `Human-authored`. Two blocks, two eyebrows.

**Edge case 2: An AI generated a table that the author edited the column headers on.**

The author edited substantively (header changes shape the table's framing). The block is `AI ← edited`. Manifest row: authorship = `<Author> ← AI`, model and tool as appropriate.

**Edge case 3: The author wrote the prose themselves but asked an AI to suggest a single phrase.**

The block is `Human-authored`. Treating it as `AI ← edited` would overstate AI involvement to the point of devaluing the category. The author's substrate includes phrase substitution at the editing stage; one suggested phrase does not flip the block's authorship.

**Edge case 4: AI translated the article into another language.**

The translation is a new article. The translation block's authorship is `AI verbatim` if untouched by a human translator, or `<Translator> ← AI` if a human translator edited the output. The original article's manifest carries forward into the translation manifest as a separate "source-article provenance" column.

**Edge case 5: The author dictated to AI which transcribed; the author then edited.**

The author is the source of the ideas; the AI is a transcription tool. Treat the block as `Human-authored`. Transcription does not constitute drafting under v1.

**Edge case 6: Multiple AI models were used on the same block.**

List the dominant model in the eyebrow and enumerate the others in the manifest row's Model column as a comma-separated list. Tool follows the same convention. The eyebrow's job is to tell the reader the category; the manifest's job is the full audit.

## What the standard does not certify

The standard certifies authorship, not accuracy. A block tagged `<Author>` may still contain factual errors that the author did not catch. A block tagged `AI verbatim` may still be factually correct.

The standard certifies the published voice, not the published opinions. A `<Author>` block is the author's voice — which the author may publish even when they disagree with the position (e.g., when writing a steelman of an opposing view).

The standard certifies block-level provenance, not paragraph-level or sentence-level provenance. Within a `<Author> ← AI` block, the human author and the AI cannot be disaggregated further; the reader treats the entire block as a hybrid product.

---

## Versioning

The standard is versioned. Articles declare their version in the "How to read this article" section near the top. Future versions may introduce additional categories (e.g., separating AI-suggested-phrase-level edits from AI-paragraph-level edits) or refine the eyebrow format.

Articles published under v1 do not need to be retroactively converted when later versions are published. The version is the contract at publication time.

---

## Compliance check

An article is v1-compliant if:

1. Every block has an italic eyebrow declaring its authorship category
2. Every AI-involved eyebrow names model provider, model name, and tool
3. A "How to read this article" section appears near the top, declaring the v1 version
4. A content provenance manifest appears at the article footer
5. Definitions of the three categories are reproduced in the article, not just linked
6. The author's published name appears in every `<Author>` eyebrow and matches the author of record

A skill or tool that produces output claiming v1 compliance is responsible for all six points. Partial compliance is non-compliance.
